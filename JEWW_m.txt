**The Meanest Meanie of Meadowbrook**

In the village of Meadowbrook, where flowers bloomed year-round and neighbors shared their bread, there lived a creature so thoroughly unpleasant that even the clouds avoided casting shadows near his cottage.

His name was Gribble.

Gribble woke each morning with a scowl already in place, as if he'd been practicing it in his sleep. He would stomp to his window, fling it open, and shout at the sunrise for being "too bright and obnoxiously cheerful." The birds learned to sing quietly near his property, and the local children had invented seventeen different routes to school just to avoid passing his fence.

His crimes were legendary:
- He put salt in the village fountain
- He told children their drawings looked "like accidents"
- He once reported a puppy for "loitering with intent to be adorable"
- He collected compliments just to throw them away unread

But Gribble's meanness reached its peak one autumn day when he decided to steal the village's beloved weathervane—a golden rooster that had predicted rain for two hundred years. He climbed the bell tower at midnight, pried it loose, and cackled as he dragged it home.

The village woke to gray skies and no rooster. Without it, they couldn't predict the weather. Farmers didn't know when to harvest. The baker couldn't plan for sunny days to dry his special bread. Chaos erupted.

Old Mrs. Hembly, who had ignored Gribble's meanness for forty years, finally had enough. She marched to his cottage and knocked so hard the door shook.

"Gribble!" she bellowed. "I know you have it!"

"Have what?" Gribble sneered through the crack.

"The weathervane. And I know *why* you took it."

Gribble froze. "You... you do?"

"Of course. You're lonely. You've been mean so long, you've forgotten how to be anything else. And now you're angry that no one even notices you unless you cause trouble."

The door opened slightly. Gribble's scowl wavered.

"That's ridiculous," he muttered. But his voice cracked.

Mrs. Hembly softened. "Return the rooster. Come to the harvest festival. I'll teach you to make apple pie, and you can complain about how mine is better the entire time. I promise to argue back."

For the first time in decades, Gribble's face did something other than scowl. It twitched uncertainly, caught between old habits and something dangerously close to hope.

"Fine," he grumbled. "But only because your pies are probably *terrible* and someone needs to tell you."

Mrs. Hembly smiled. "I'm counting on it."

The weathervane returned to the tower that night, mysteriously, with no explanation. And on harvest festival day, the villagers were shocked to see Gribble at a pie-making table, arguing loudly with Mrs. Hembly about cinnamon measurements while she laughed and called him an "old goat."

He was still mean. But now he was *their* mean old goat.

And somehow, that made all the difference.

**THE END**
